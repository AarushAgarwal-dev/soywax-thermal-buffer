[0:00.7 - 0:19.2] Can soy wax smooth out heat spikes in compact AI hardware? This simulation compares three builds of a small AI chip with a fan heatsink: the stock heatsink, the same heatsink with 20 grams of soy wax, and that wax cast into aluminum foam. Each build runs three short AI jobs, then a long one.
[0:21.7 - 0:28.9] Each card on the left is one build. At idle, all three sit near 37 degrees Celsius, and the wax is solid.

[1:02.0 - 1:11.5] Job two. The plain wax pack helps much less. Wax conducts heat poorly, so only the first 1.8 millimeters of its 9 millimeter slab melts.
[1:14.2 - 1:23.4] Job three. Across all three short jobs, the foam build never throttles, while the stock heatsink throttles for about 44 seconds of every 90.
[1:26.2 - 1:28.3] Now, an 8 minute job at full load.
[1:28.7 - 1:34.8] The stock heatsink throttles after 52 seconds, and the plain wax build after 71 seconds.

[1:43.6 - 1:50.7] From here, it settles to the same 19 watts as the stock heatsink. Wax buys time, but adds no sustained cooling.
[1:51.2 - 1:58.0] Re-solidifying after a long job is slow. 5 minutes later, the foam pack is still 64 percent molten.
[2:00.6 - 2:25.9] The scorecard. Over the three short jobs, the stock heatsink throttled for 131 seconds. Plain wax cut that to 47, no better than the same weight of plain aluminum. The foam build never throttled, and peaked 9 degrees cooler. So: match the melting point to your hardware, keep all the wax within about 2 millimeters of metal, and expect it to buy minutes, not extra cooling. The next step is a bench test.
